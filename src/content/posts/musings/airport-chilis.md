My flight home was originally scheduled for a 5:55 PM departure back to Tampa, and naturally it was already delayed by two hours. As one does, the first thing you do once you get past security is confirm that your gate does exist in the airport, and then find a snack or use the bathroom, and so on. Unfortunately, this particular flight, and I, drew the short straw. The gate agent announced over the PA that the crew was still on its way in from Denver to Illinois, where I was currently sitting. The board ticked back another hour, and just as I looked up from messaging my partner about the delay, it already read 8:55 PM. I had spent the last few days on a site visit in a place called Itasca, a town built almost exclusively out of office parks and Hampton Inns, and I had been just about ready to get home since roughly 1:45 PM on that Monday. So I did what a great many Americans have done in exactly this position, which was pull up the handle on my roller bag and walk the concourse towards the airport Chili's.

Now, there's a major case against it, and we can get that sorted up front. A draft beer costs roughly what a six-pack does at the local gas station, and every price on the menu is slightly jarring, but in the airport, prices reflect a captive audience, so, c'est la vie, as they say. Sometimes you may find yourself waiting at the front host stand for a table that you can see has been empty the entire time from where you're standing, and yes, sometimes it's so packed to the gills you simply have to admit defeat and walk away. People who like to sound more worldly and traveled, and who presumably have 500+ posts on their Instagram, will call it just a chain food spot with no charm or redeeming qualities, but that's just an untrained eye looking at the surface of an institution staring them in the face.

Do yourself a favor and skip the host stand and the tables – go right to the bar. Roller bag under the stool, jacket onto the hook under the counter, and before you know it the bartender has dropped a square napkin in front of you before you've said a word, and you're in. I don't need a menu at this point (rest in peace to physical menus, and I wish nothing but the worst for the QR code menus), as the options are always the same. Sometimes it's just a burger, sometimes the Triple Dipper (mozz sticks, crispers, wings) lined up with sauces down the middle, the closest you'll get to a tasting menu in the place. You may even catch a glimpse of the heralded, much-commented-on fajita skillet being delivered to a table, a spectacle that rivals 4th of July fireworks for the way people stop and stare.

That sameness is what I go there for. Travel has a way of taking away almost every kind of control a grown adult is accustomed to having, since someone else decides when you're leaving, where you're sitting, how much your bag can weigh, and whether you end up going at all. None of it is up to you, and most of it is up to a system that's probably still running Windows XP. Against all of that, there is something to be said for the real comfort of a room where you know exactly what you're going to get, and how it's going to be delivered.

There are only a couple dozen or so airport Chili's in the US, and I've managed to dine at six of them over the years. I can recall ordering the Crispers years ago and having them come out on a metal tray like a tiny baking sheet, with the buffalo sauce (I don't do honey mustard, sorry) in its own small metal cup and a full ear of corn lying alongside, back when they still gave you the whole ear. I can tell you that the chicken came out too hot and burned my mouth, and that the Sprite was almost as sharp as a McDonald's Sprite, but I can't even begin to guess where I was at the time.

There's something deeply American about the whole arrangement, and that's meant as a compliment. Chili's started as a burger place in Dallas in 1975, and somewhere along the way it became one of those fixtures, like the Waffle House sign at the interstate exit, that tells you what country you're in. Then we took that fixture and set it down inside one of the real marvels of the modern world, a building whose entire purpose is to launch people across the continent at five hundred miles an hour, and filled it with people for whom that marvel has become a commute. The man two stools down from me flies to Columbus twice a month the way his grandfather probably drove to his factory or plant, and before he gets carried over three states in a pressurized tube he is going to eat mozzarella sticks under a muted college football game (it's a replay from 2005) with the captions running a couple of seconds behind.

On a weeknight, most of the bar patrons are people like him and me, flying alone and on the way back from somewhere they probably didn't want to go to. There's the consultant who has been in the corner seat since before noon, laptop open and lanyard still on from a site visit. There's another in a corporate quarter-zip finishing a call he has clearly been dreading, mouthing "sorry" to the waiter who has come to clear his plate. There's the woman at the end with a paperback and a skillet of queso all to herself. And there's nearly always one family who has given up on the delay and decided to treat the layover as a small vacation, tablets on the table, the kids splitting a Molten Chocolate Cake by the singular window while the parents sit behind their margaritas with a slightly "dying inside" expression as their flight pushes back another hour.

What surprises me every time is how much talking goes on. Everyone at the bar is stuck in the same way, and that seems to be enough to get people going, starting with the only two questions anyone ever asks in an airport, where you headed and where you coming from, and then wandering off into hometown teams and kids and the relative merits of the food before them. Someone checks the airline app and tells the whole bar that Charlotte has slipped again to 9:10, and there's a small, sympathetic groan from three people who didn't know each other an hour ago, now commiserating together over a common enemy. It's a room full of strangers from all over the country eating the same thing they could have eaten at home, and that's exactly why it feels like a bit of a break, which we could all use these days.

An airport is designed, very deliberately, to keep you moving: through security, past the shops, to the gate, down the jet bridge and into your seat. Almost nothing in the building is meant for staying put, as anyone who has tried to lie down across gate seats with welded-in armrests overnight can tell you. The airport Chili's is one of the few places in the whole terminal that lets you sit at a counter, eat a meal with a real fork and be a person for forty minutes instead of a passenger, and I've come to think that's most of what the check is paying for.

Eventually, everyone gets on their flight, whether it's that day or the next. You settle up with your bartender, or your waiter, leave a little more of a tip than necessary for the kindness they showed you during your visit, and step off the stool. You give a nod to those around you, all with similar journeys ahead of or behind them, and to the next delayed traveler waiting by the host stand, who is almost assuredly going to slide right onto the very same stool before it ever has a chance to go cold.
